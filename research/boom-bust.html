Boom-Bust Capital Flow Cycles

Zheyu Yang and Graciela L. Kaminsky · Working Paper · 2024

Why do international capital flows surge into a country, and why do those surges end? This paper studies China's participation in global capital markets during the first era of financial globalization, from the end of the Napoleonic Wars to the Great Depression. During this period, Chinese public borrowers and private firms tapped the major financial centers of the time—London, Paris, Frankfurt, Berlin, and New York—to finance government needs as well as railways, navigation, banking, and other commercial activities.

The paper uses this historical setting to examine the forces behind capital-flow bonanzas. Were inflows to China mainly driven by "push" factors, such as abundant international liquidity and investors' search for yield in global financial centers? Or were they driven by "pull" factors, such as changes in China's own economic prospects, institutions, and demand for external finance? By distinguishing these mechanisms, the paper connects China's historical borrowing episodes to a broader question in international finance: when do capital inflows reflect productive opportunities, and when do they signal vulnerability?

We also study how these inflow booms ended and what they left behind. Did foreign capital support growth by financing infrastructure and enterprise development, or did it increase exposure to sudden stops, financial distress, and crisis? The project brings a long historical perspective to modern debates over capital-flow cycles, showing that the tension between external liquidity, domestic fundamentals, and financial fragility is not new.
